Data storytelling · Exercise 3

One TV dataset, three storyboards

We looked at 4,508 television models currently available for sale in Australia, comparing screen size against labelled yearly energy use. The same numbers answer very different questions depending on who's asking, so below are three short, self-contained stories — each planned as its own six-panel storyboard before a single chart was built, and each written for a different reader.

Story 1 · Six-panel storyboard

Does a bigger TV really cost more to run?

Written for TV shoppers comparing models before they buy — people who care about the sticker price, but rarely check what a screen costs to keep switched on.

Storyboard

1 · Issue

Shoppers focus on the price tag, not what a TV costs to run each year

2 · Demonstrate issue

Scatter plot of screen size vs. yearly kWh shows a strong upward trend

3 · Idea

Group TVs into small / medium / large so shoppers can compare running costs

4 · Describe

Small ≈128 kWh, medium ≈383 kWh, large ≈749 kWh a year on average

5 · Evidence

Yearly running cost: $38 vs $115 vs $225 — nearly 6× from small to large

6 · Recommendation

Check the running-cost calculator before you buy — size isn't just a screen decision

Every dot on the chart below is one television model. Follow the cloud of dots and a clear shape appears: as screens grow, so does their yearly energy appetite. Using an indicative electricity price of 30¢ per kWh, that gap is worth real money — a small TV costs about $38 a year to run; a large one, around $225.

Chart — screen size vs. yearly energy use

Each point is one of the 4,508 TV models currently available for sale in Australia. Source: tv_2026_02_15.csv.

0.86 correlation between screen size and yearly energy use

What this means for you

Screen size on its own explains most of the difference in running cost — you don't need to know anything else about a model to make a fair guess at its yearly bill.

Story 2 · Six-panel storyboard

Where should energy efficiency rules focus?

Written for policymakers and regulators deciding where efficiency standards would have the biggest impact on national energy use.

Storyboard

1 · Issue

Large screens are booming, quietly pushing up national energy demand

2 · Demonstrate issue

Line chart: average energy use rises, and accelerates, past ~75"

3 · Idea

Target efficiency standards where energy use is growing fastest

4 · Describe

32"→55" adds ~246 kWh, but 75"→100" adds ~497 kWh — almost double

5 · Evidence

The curve steepens sharply — large TVs carry an outsized share of total energy use

6 · Recommendation

Prioritise stricter efficiency standards for 75"+ TVs as the market shifts bigger

The relationship isn't a straight line — it curves upward. Energy use climbs faster once screens pass the 75"–85" mark than it does between, say, 32" and 55". As larger screens become more popular with buyers, that acceleration matters more each year.

Chart — average energy use at every screen size

Notice how the curve steepens after ~75". Source: tv_2026_02_15.csv.

2× the energy jump from 75"→100" vs. 32"→55"

Why this matters

Because the curve accelerates, the very biggest screens carry an outsized share of the market's total energy use — a useful signal for where efficiency standards matter most.

Story 3 · Six-panel storyboard

Is screen size the whole story?

Written for researchers and analysts studying energy efficiency in consumer electronics, who want the nuance behind the headline correlation.

Storyboard

1 · Issue

"Bigger screen = always worse" is an oversimplification of the data

2 · Demonstrate issue

Correlation is strong (0.86) but not perfect — size alone doesn't decide everything

3 · Idea

Look at the spread within each size category, not just the average

4 · Describe

Typical range widens sharply with size: small ≈83–173 kWh, large ≈483–1015 kWh

5 · Evidence

Some large TVs sit closer to a medium TV's usage than to other large models

6 · Recommendation

Worth investigating panel technology and brand as secondary factors, not size alone

A correlation of 0.86 is strong, but it leaves room for real variation. A large TV's typical range is over 500 kWh/year wide — far wider than a small TV's — so "large" doesn't automatically mean "wasteful."

If you want a big screen, it's worth comparing specific models rather than ruling out a whole size category — some large TVs are more efficient than others by a wide margin.

About this analysis

Data & processing: figures on this page come from the Australian Government's household appliance energy rating dataset (tv_2026_02_15.csv), filtered to models marked "Available" and sold in Australia, then processed in KNIME to convert screen size from cm to inches and group models into small / medium / large categories — mirroring the Exercise 2 workflow. Each of the three stories above was planned as its own six-panel storyboard before any chart was built.

Limitations: the electricity price used for dollar estimates (30¢/kWh) is an indicative average, not a specific plan or region. Labelled energy consumption is a standardised test figure and may differ from real-world usage.
