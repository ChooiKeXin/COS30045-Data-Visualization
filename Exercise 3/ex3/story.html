Data storytelling · Exercise 3

Does a bigger TV really cost you more?

We looked at 4,508 television models currently available for sale in Australia — comparing screen size against labelled yearly energy use — to find out. The story unfolds in three short chapters below, each built for a different reader: someone shopping for a TV, someone setting energy policy, and someone who just wants to see what the data really says.

Chapter 1

Bigger screen, bigger bill

Every dot on the chart below is one television model. Its position left-to-right shows screen size in inches; its position bottom-to-top shows how many kilowatt-hours it's labelled to use in a year. Follow the cloud of dots and a clear shape appears: as screens grow, so does their yearly energy appetite.

Each point is one of the 4,508 TV models currently available for sale in Australia (screen size converted from cm to inches). Source: tv_2026_02_15.csv.

What this means

A correlation of 0.86 (out of a possible 1.0) is strong. In plain terms: screen size on its own explains most of the difference in how much energy a TV uses each year — you don't need to know anything else about a model to make a fair guess at its running cost.

Chapter 3

Diminishing returns: is bigger always worse?

The relationship isn't a straight line — it curves upward. Energy use climbs faster once screens pass the 75"–85" mark than it does between, say, 32" and 55". And not every large TV is equally hungry: the bigger the screen, the wider the spread between the most and least efficient models.

Chart 3 — Average energy use at every screen size

Notice how the curve steepens after ~75". Going from 32" to 55" adds roughly 246 kWh/year, but going from 75" to 100" adds around 497 kWh/year — nearly double the jump for a similar step up in size.

Why this matters

Because the curve accelerates, the very biggest screens carry an outsized share of the market's total energy use — a useful signal for regulators thinking about where efficiency standards matter most.

The nuance

A large TV's typical range is over 500 kWh/year wide — far wider than a small TV's. So "large" doesn't automatically mean "wasteful": some large models sit closer to a medium TV's usage. If you want a big screen, it's worth comparing specific models rather than ruling out a whole size category.

What this means for you

Three chapters, three audiences — here's the takeaway for each.

C

Consumers

Screen size is the single biggest driver of a TV's running cost. If energy bills matter to you, weigh up the extra $100–$200 a year a bigger screen can add — then use our calculator to check a specific model.

P

Policymakers & regulators

Energy use accelerates sharply above ~75". As larger screens become more popular, efficiency standards targeted at this segment could have an outsized impact on nationwide consumption.

R

Researchers

The strong overall correlation (0.86) hides real variability within each size category — a reminder that screen size is a good predictor, but not the whole story. Panel technology and other factors are worth a closer look.

About this analysis

Data & processing: figures on this page come from the Australian Government's household appliance energy rating dataset (tv_2026_02_15.csv), filtered to models marked "Available" and sold in Australia, then processed in KNIME to convert screen size from cm to inches and group models into small / medium / large categories — mirroring the Exercise 2 workflow.

Limitations: the electricity price used for dollar estimates (30¢/kWh) is an indicative average, not a specific plan or region. Labelled energy consumption is a standardised test figure and may differ from real-world usage.
